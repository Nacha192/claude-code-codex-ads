# Retention: why people keep watching

The mechanics of holding attention in a paid video, and how they differ when
you are selling a product, capturing a lead, or selling a service.

This file is about the **structure**, not the prompt. A perfectly generated shot
in the wrong position loses the viewer exactly as fast as a bad one.

---

## The three windows

Attention is not lost gradually. It is lost at three specific moments, and each
has a different fix.

| Window | What decides it | Failure looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **0.0 – 0.5 s** | the first frame, before any word is read | nobody starts. `early.hook_rate` collapses |
| **0.5 – 3 s** | the promise: what question is now open | they start and drop at 2 s |
| **3 s – end** | the payoff rhythm: does something keep arriving | `early.hold_rate` collapses while hook holds |

### 0 to 0.5 seconds — the scroll-stop

Before anything is understood, something must physically stop the thumb. Five
things do it, and only five:

1. **Motion.** A fast movement, a whip-pan, something falling or breaking.
2. **A face with direct eye contact**, or an unusual expression.
3. **A visual anomaly.** Wrong scale, wrong colour, wrong object in a place.
   "That is strange" registers before "that is an ad".
4. **Text already on screen at frame one**, carrying a claim.
5. **A recognisable sound**, for the minority with sound on. Never rely on it.

Name which of the five your first frame uses. If the honest answer is "none",
the video does not have an opening; it has a beginning.

**The first frame is a decision.** Choose it. Do not let the encoder pick it.

### 0.5 to 3 seconds — the open question

The viewer is not asking "is this interesting". They are asking "is this about
me". The hook's job is to answer yes, fast, and leave one thing unresolved.

See `hooks.md` for the three channels and the formula library. Two rules that
belong here rather than there:

- **The question must be answerable by the video.** An open loop the video never
  closes buys a view and loses the click.
- **Situation, not person.** See `compliance.md`. This is a policy rule and a
  craft rule at once.

### 3 seconds to the end — the payoff rhythm

At 3 seconds the platform has counted a view and the viewer is deciding again.
This is where most ads die, and they die of the same thing: **second four
repeats second one.**

Something must arrive by 0:03. A cut, new information, a second hook, visible
progress. A logo, a slow pan, or "hey guys" is a scroll.

After that, the rhythm that holds:

- **A cut every 1 to 2 seconds** for the first ten seconds, in UGC and
  rapid-fire. A six-second static frame is a scroll.
- **A pattern interrupt roughly every 3 to 5 seconds**: a zoom, a text pop, a
  sound effect, a B-roll insert, a speed ramp. Each one re-grabs a wandering
  eye.
- **One open loop at a time**, closed before the next opens. Two unresolved
  questions read as confusion, not intrigue.
- **The dead zone.** Every draft has one. Find it by watching and noticing where
  you check the timeline. Cut it. It is almost always between the proof and the
  offer.

## The mute test and the blind test

Run both, on the storyboard, before anything is shot or generated.

**Mute it.** Do you understand it and stay? Feed autoplay is muted, so this is
most of your impressions. If it fails, the video does not work, however good the
voice-over is.

**Blacken the screen.** Would the audio alone hold you? If yes, it is
audio-driven, which is rare in paid social and usually a mistake.

Then state the split you are aiming for, as a number: "70 % visual, 30 % audio,
the burned-in captions carry it and the music only sets pace". This single
sentence is the most transferable thing you can write about a video ad, and it
decides your whole production budget.

## Three objectives, three different videos

The most common mistake in a video brief is treating these as one job. They
recruit different viewers, hold them for different reasons, and end differently.

### Product

**The viewer must believe the object does the thing.**

- The proof is a **demonstration**, and it is the centre of the video, not the
  end. Show it working, once, clearly, in real time. A demonstration that needs
  a cut to work is not a demonstration.
- **Show the object early.** The moment they see it, they become product-aware,
  which means the rest of the video must handle objections rather than build
  mystery. See `copywriting.md`.
- Length: **15 to 30 seconds** is the band where this holds.
- Ends on: the object, the offer, one action.
- Kills it: hiding the product for 20 seconds, then revealing something ordinary.

### Lead

**The viewer must believe the problem is theirs and the next step is small.**

- The proof is **recognition**, not demonstration. They should feel seen before
  they are offered anything.
- Never show the form. The video sells the conversation, not the paperwork.
- **The offer is information**, and it is free: the guide, the audit, the call.
  Naming the price of the real service in a lead video kills the lead.
- Length: **20 to 45 seconds**. It needs room to build recognition.
- Ends on: one small, concrete next step, stated once.
- Kills it: qualifying too hard too early. Every disqualifier in the first ten
  seconds costs you leads you wanted.

### Service

**The viewer must believe a competent person is on the other end.**

This is the one people get wrong, because they copy product ads.

- The proof is **specificity about the work**, not about the outcome. "We
  rebuilt the booking flow and the no-show rate is in the case study" beats "we
  grow businesses" by an enormous margin.
- **A face helps here more than anywhere else.** A service is bought from a
  person. An anonymous service ad is a brochure.
- Show **the work**, not the result: the screen, the site, the room, the tool in
  hand. Result-only reads as a claim.
- Length: **30 to 60 seconds**, and it earns it if the specificity is real.
- Ends on: a conversation, not a purchase.
- Kills it: stock footage of a handshake, an office, or a team meeting. It
  signals "no real work to show" faster than saying so.

### The table

| | Product | Lead | Service |
|---|---|---|---|
| Viewer must believe | the object works | the problem is mine | the person is competent |
| Proof is | a demonstration | recognition | specificity about the work |
| Show early | the object | the situation | the person and the work |
| Length band | 15–30 s | 20–45 s | 30–60 s |
| Ends on | the offer | a small next step | a conversation |
| Fatal | a hidden ordinary product | early over-qualification | stock footage |

## Watching to the end

Completion matters less than people think for a feed ad and more than they think
for a lead or service ad, where the call to action lives at the end.

What actually produces completion:

- **A stated duration or count.** "Three things" or "in 20 seconds" gives the
  viewer a contract and a reason to stay for the third.
- **A payoff promised at the start and delivered at the end**, with one
  reference in the middle to remind them it is coming.
- **Escalating stakes**, not repetition. Each beat must be more interesting than
  the last, or the curve sags and they leave at the sag.
- **A last beat worth reaching.** If the ending is the logo, nobody reaches it,
  and your call to action is on the part nobody sees. Put the offer at the last
  interesting moment, not after it.

What does not: a countdown timer, "wait for it" with a thin payoff, an artificial
cliffhanger. Viewers have learned all three.

## Reading it afterwards

Two failures, two different rewrites. See `thresholds.md`.

- `early.hook_rate` low → the opening does not stop the scroll. New shot, new
  line. Keep the body.
- `early.hold_rate` low while hook holds → the body does not pay off the
  opening. New body. Keep the hook.

Compare inside `early.duration_band`, never across it. And remember
`early.false_negative_rate`: roughly one in five creatives cut on early signals
would have gone on to win. That is the price of throughput, and the output says
so.

## What this file is not

It is not a claim about any specific platform's ranking system. Nobody outside
those companies knows how the ranking works, and the people who say they do are
selling something.

Everything above is about **the viewer**, which is observable, stable across
platforms, and the only part you control.
