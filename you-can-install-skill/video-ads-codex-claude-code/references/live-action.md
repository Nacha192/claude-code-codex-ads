# Real production: filming it

The strongest video ad on most accounts is still a real person, filming a real
product, on a phone. Not because generated footage is bad, but because a
demonstration is proof and a rendering is an illustration. A viewer who has
been sold to before can tell which one they are watching, and the difference
shows up in hold rate long before it shows up in cost per acquisition.

This file is what to do when the answer is "film it". It is written for a phone
and one person, because that is the real constraint, and it scales up without
changing shape.

---

## When filming beats generating

| Situation | Why |
|---|---|
| The product does something visible | The demonstration is the ad. Nothing recreates it honestly. |
| Trust is the obstacle | A face that takes responsibility for a claim carries it. A synthetic one carries nothing. |
| The category is saturated with polished ads | Deliberately unpolished reads as native and stops the scroll that gloss does not. |
| The offer is local or service-based | The place, the van, the workshop and the hands are the proof. |
| Fidelity matters: labels, colorways, materials, scale | A generator will drift on all four. |

**When not to.** No product in hand, no consenting person, an impossible or
dangerous scene, or a concept whose whole point is that it could not be filmed.
Say which, and route to the generation path instead.

---

## Before anyone is filmed: identity and rights

Nothing is shot until this is written down. Not because of caution, because of
the cost of discovering it later, when the ad is running and someone asks to be
removed from it.

**The identity release.** Written, from every identifiable person, before the
shoot. It names:

- Who is filmed, and who is filming.
- **Where it will run**: which platforms, which ad accounts, by name.
- **For how long**, as absolute dates, not "ongoing".
- **Which territories.**
- **Whether it may be edited**, recut, subtitled or dubbed.
- **What happens on withdrawal**: how they ask, what gets taken down, how fast.

A creator who hands you rushes has not thereby given you an identity release.
Those are two different permissions and they are refused separately.

**Minors, employees, customers and bystanders.** A minor needs the guardian's
release, not their own. An employee's presence in an ad is not covered by their
employment. A customer filmed in a shop is a bystander until they sign. If a
bystander is identifiable and has not signed, they are cut or blurred, and that
decision is made in the edit, not argued about after publication.

**Voice is a separate right from face.** See the pack's voice file. Recording
someone speaking does not authorize cloning their voice.

## The creator brief

What you send someone who will film without you. Its only job is to make the
rushes usable, so it is short, specific, and never a mood board.

1. **One sentence on who is being spoken to**, in their words, not in marketing
   language.
2. **The exact spoken lines**, if there are any, written the way they are said
   out loud. Mark which words are mandatory, legally or otherwise, and which
   they may rephrase in their own vocabulary. The second list should be longer.
3. **The shot list below**, with what must be visible in each.
4. **The product truth**: what may be shown, what may not be claimed, per
   [compliance](compliance.md).
5. **The technical floor**: vertical, landscape lock off, highest resolution the
   phone offers, clean lens, no filters, no in-app beautification, no platform
   watermark. A watermarked export is unusable as an ad.
6. **The register**, per [intake](intake.md), described as three things to do and
   three to avoid, not as an adjective.
7. **What you will not use**: so the reshoot conversation is already had.

## Mandatory coverage

The single most common failure is rushes that cannot be edited. Not bad
footage, missing footage. This list is what makes a cut possible at all.

| Shot | Why it is mandatory |
|---|---|
| Hook take, three separate versions | The first second decides everything and you cannot fix it in the edit |
| The demonstration, once, unbroken, no cut | A cut in the middle of a demonstration reads as a hidden edit and destroys the proof |
| Product alone, held, turning slowly | The only shot that survives every recut |
| Hands doing the thing, close | Carries the product truth when the wide shot cannot |
| The result, held long enough to read | Cutting away from the result is cutting away from the reason to buy |
| Two seconds of silence, room tone | Without it, no clean audio edit is possible |
| The CTA line, spoken and silent | You will need both, because most viewers are muted |
| Twenty seconds of B-roll around the scene | The material that saves the edit when a take is unusable |

**Safety takes.** Every mandatory shot is filmed at least twice, from a slightly
different distance. The second take costs thirty seconds on set and saves a
reshoot.

## On the day: the four things that ruin footage

**Light.** A window in front of the subject, never behind. Overcast daylight is
the best free light there is. Mixed light, a window plus a ceiling bulb, gives
a colour cast that no correction fully removes.

**Sound.** The phone microphone at arm's length is acceptable. Across a room is
not. Film away from a fridge, a road and an air vent, and record room tone.
Bad audio is the one defect a viewer will not forgive, and it cannot be
repaired.

**Framing.** Vertical, subject slightly off centre, head not cropped, and
nothing important in the bottom fifth or the top eighth where the platform puts
its own interface. Check the pack's assembly file for the safe zones.

**Continuity.** Same clothes, same background, same product state across takes
that will sit next to each other. The most expensive continuity error is a
product that is full in one shot and half empty in the next.

## Delivery of the rushes

Ask for, and check on arrival:

- **Original files**, not a chat-app re-encode. A clip that arrived through a
  messaging app has already lost the quality you are about to compress again.
- **Every take**, including the ones the creator thinks are bad. They are often
  the natural ones.
- **A file naming scheme** that says which shot each file is.
- **The signed release**, before the edit starts, not before publication.

On arrival, inspect before editing: duration, resolution, frame rate, audio
present on every clip. Use the pack's measurable checks. Discovering at the
edit that half the clips are silent is a reshoot; discovering it on arrival is
a message.

---

## Reshoot criteria

Reshoot, do not rescue, when:

- The audio is unusable on the spoken take.
- The demonstration was cut mid-action.
- The product shown is the wrong version, colorway or state.
- A mandatory legal term is absent from a take where it is required.
- An identifiable person has not signed.

Rescue in the edit, do not reshoot, when the problem is pacing, order,
framing within a larger frame, colour, or a hook that exists but sits at
0:14 instead of 0:00.

---

## What goes into the delivery

The rushes are not the ad. The edit follows the pack's assembly file, and the
delivery carries, in addition to the usual channels: the shot list as filmed,
the release on file with its expiry date, the reshoot list if any, and the
usage window after which this ad must be pulled or renegotiated.

**Put the expiry date in the campaign record.** An ad running past the end of
its release is the most common rights failure in this whole craft, and it
happens because nobody wrote the date anywhere a human would see it again.
